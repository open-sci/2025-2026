      Silvio: During the lecture (Open Infrastructures, see https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1Dh2Uv7g0DydXabvS6FP2QOYx1I1s7TsvmXsfPVRXXgc/edit?usp=sharing), the class have discussed the content of the following paper:
      • Elliott, S., & Sterner, B. (2025). How Open Science organizations generate epistemic oppression. European Journal for Philosophy of Science, 15(3), 47. https://doi.org/10.1007/s13194-025-00674-0
      The goal of today's discussion is to define a table that can be used by Open Science Infrastructure Directors as a guideline to identify possible risks that may generate epistemic oppression in such Open Science organisation, accompanied by the possible actions to adopt to mitigate such risks. The entire class is asked to proceed, at the very beginning, organising an open discussion, without mediation by the professor and without to pre-organise groups, to start identifying the primary risks/issues and then to organise the work to do. The table should be completed in the following 40 minutes of work, and it is represented as follows in a form of a list.

      Members of the class: Tommaso, Miriana, Maryam, Nicol, Peize, YiHua, Regina, Shiho, Ilaria, Chen

      1. Risk: Organizational Path Dependency
      Example: Early infrastructure decisions become hard to change because subsequent tools and workflows depend from them.
      Action: periodic review of standards from multiple points of view (including external actors)

      2. Risk: No mechanisms of contesting standards, so external communities usually end up creating their own
      Example: Harmed users don't have a way to challenge policies, rules, classifications etc...
      Action: Create an appeal process ensuring representation from affected groups

      3. Risk: Learning mechanisms becoming a barrier to the adoption of inclusive standards
      Example: If an elite group of powerful individuals has already mastered highly technical standards, the motivation to explore new options decreases, maybe due to the cost and effort of retraining.
      Action: Allocate dedicated funding for training programs and learning resources so members can effectively adopt newly introduced standards.

      4. Risk: Stakeholders' guide decisions and data practices decided by them, often erase the role of Indigenous Peoples in shaping landscapes by failing to account for traditional ecological knowledge.
      Example: Infrastructure decisions might ignore the local community's classifications/languages disempowering them from their own knowledge
      Action:  Incorporating the indigenous people's classification and language and empowering their authority over the terms of data access and use.

      5. Risk: Single standards lock-in. “Whoever has the power sets the taxonomy, using their science… And this does not appear to be changing any time soon!” (Steiner & Huettmann, 2023, 35).
      Example: Single taxonomy/schema/classification might make data usable for one group while excluding others.
      Action: Support plural standards where possible

      6. Risk: Centralization of knowledge
      Example: even if science is open, it is still managed by very few big institutions that decide on the organisation of data
      Action: Merging of Knowledge Methodology - https://engageplus.org/en/croisement-des-savoirs.asp

      7. Risk: Cumulative advantage for dominant users
      Example : eminent scientists tend to receive greater credit for their work in comparison to less eminent scientists, especially in cases of co-authored publications and simultaneous discoveries.
      Action: track benefit distribution, avoid giving higher salience to already-dominant stakeholder groups when setting their policies

      8. Risk: Denied transfer of legal authority over physical or digital records related to specific communities
      Example: Brazilian researchers have created digital repatriation portals for specimens of their area but the physical ones remain in custody outside Brazilian herbaria, even when digital information about them was repatriated.
      Action: Perhaps we could introduce decentralised authority and ownership of scientific objects (physical or digital) by using a blockchain infrastructure to give equal control over scientific material

      9. Risk: Sharing data requires the use and management of collective conceptual resources that are not neutral with respect to different goals pursued by contributors and users of databases.
      Example: Such as enforcing a uniform biological classification for all species observations in a database.
      Action: By emphasizing the importance of organizational policies about data standardization.

      10. Risk: Ignoring language barriers
      Example: Monolanguage infrastructures privileges dominant groups reducing partecipations from local communities
      Action: Provide multi-language infrastructures/classifications/documentation, prioritizing languages from those local communities.

      • Waltman, L. (2025, March 26). Assessment reform and publishing reform need to go hand in hand. Front Matter. https://doi.org/10.59350/2pxgd-tj976 

      The goal of today's discussion is concerning the creation of a set of guidelines to provide to UNIBO evaluators to assess the people (professors and researchers) working at the University of Bologna. In particular, each person should be assessed according to three different dimensions:
      • publications - i.e. the publication that one publishes, independently from their types;
      • teachings - i.e. the grade of teaching that one has to fulfill for his/her institutional mandates;
      • other aspects from the CV - i.e. all other relevant activities, which do not relates directly with publications and teachings, that one usually address during his/her univeristy activities.

      The class is split in three different groups, i.e. Group Publications, Group Teachings, Group Other, each caring about one of these dimentions. For each dimension, each group has to indentify guidelines and assessment rules to follow, considering that the assessement should "rely primarily on qualitative expert judgment, with a supporting role for quantitative indicators" (Waltman, 2025). Each group should spend around 30 minutes to elaborate a set of guidelines and rules for its dimension. The result of this analysis is described as follows.

      Group Publications (members: Peize, Tianchi, Yigit, Yi Hua)
      • Composition and workflow: 
        •  An open workflow
          • Data Group first issue an objective report on the "openness" of the scholar.
          • Based on this report, the Domain Expert Group should consist 5-7 members: 3-4 industry experts, 1 consultant of open science, 1 Discipline Impact Assessor, evaluates the scientific value and practical contribution to the academic community in conjunction with the "Narrative CV" submitted by the candidates.
        • A constructive feedback loop
          • The Committee needed to produce a short evaluation report about the researcher's contribution and areas for improvement
      • Data collection: avoid using limited or expensive dataset, but use open academic infrastructure like ORCID to track the output of a researcher 
      • Assessment 
        • Qualitative Assessment
          • The publication should provide open access to the underlying data and code
          • The author should publish it first as a Preprint to accelerate access to ideas
          • The publication should feature Open Peer Review reports that evaluators can actually read to understand the consensus of other experts in the field
        • Quantitative Support
          • The percentage of the candidate’s portfolio that is published Gold/Diamond Open Access; 
          • The number of datasets or code repositories linked to their publications that have been downloaded or forked by the community.

          The discussion session of this lecture is organised as a role playing game. The professor acts as the editor-in-chief of a new journal about Digital Humanities that will be launched in two months, and there is the need to choosing a precise peer review model to adopt for the journal itself. In order to have a support in this decision, the editor-in-chief asked to three groups of experts (created randomly balancing the number of students per groups) in three different reviwing systems - i.e. pre-publication double-blind, registered report, publish-review-curate - to highlights what are the strenght of each of the considered peer reviewing process. The goal is to have enough material to discuss all of these to the editorial board of the journal and, finally, take a final informed decision.

          Group A (pre-publication double-blind) - members: Miriana, Sara, Shiho, Yi Hua
          Strength of the peer review model:
          Bias is minimalized because the anonymity of both authors and reviewers is secured.
          - Prestige bias is prevented (previous careers, accomplishments, publications, etc. are not considered by the reviewer)
          - Avoid interest conflicts and protect reviewers from possible negative consequences on their careers (e.g. a negative review of a famous author' publication could affect the carreer of a young reviewer) 
          - Gender and ethnicity bias are less likely to be applied
          - Protecting anonymity permits to focus on the quality and content of the research outcome 
          - The authors and reviewers are familiar to this model because it has been used for years in both science and humanities field.
            Group B (registered report) - members: Tommaso, Peize, Chen, Yiğit
            Strength of the peer review model:
            Two-stage evaluation focusing on study protocol before data collection
            • Allows early feedback before data collection by having a review of the methodology before the final empirical research is carried out allowing researchers to refine their study design or data collection protocols, this way enhancing research quality
            • Allows methodological rigour and reproducibility, forcing researchers to clearly specify hypotheses, variables, measurements and methods
            • Reduces publication biases by focusing less on positive outcomes of a research since acceptance is decided before results are known and publication of the results is guaranteed no matter the actual outcome (e.g. null or negative)

            Group C (publish-review-curate) - members: Regina, Maryam, Ilaria, Nicol
            Strength of the peer review model:
            - transparency and accessibility of the whole process 
            - supporting different points of view from independent reviewers, minimizing biases
            - no gatekeeping of publishing or delays of dissemination
            - cost and time efficiency
            - open platforms allow discussion and exchange of ideas
            - no constriction or suppression of ideas, making it more democratic and less conservative, open for innovations and creativity
            - open repositories allow long-term preservation of the product and process through version history
            - pre-prints methods are good for publishing papers about methodology 
            - this model may also be more flexible for digital humanities research which may produce more subjective research that would benefit from a wider range of perspectives for reviewing

            * publish-review-curate:
                - "noise" in the review: a public discussion could result in too many comments which could prevent a correct evaluation
                - can lead to confusion of the author, reviews of inequity and exclusivity
                - non reliable, not scientifically proven articles could be published and spread fake information

            Cons of the other peer revewing models:
            * pre-publication double-blind:
            • Anonymity is imperfect as often authors background could be inferred by key elements of their writing style/content/references

            • It favors mainstream paradigms and hinders the novelty in the discipline

            The goal is to have, for each work, a precise definition about how "reproducibility" has been detailed, adding at least one running example for each level of reproducibility identified. The groups and their outputs are detailed as follows:
                
            Group A: Miriana, Nicol, Maryam, working on Goodman et al. ([17] in the article above)
            • Methods Reproducibiliity: it consists in exactly reproducing a study by using the same raw data and the same methodologies to obtain the same results. However whether a particular study can be considered methodologically reproducible is contingent on the level of detail provided and on the existence of general agreement about what level of detail is sufficient. 
              • Example: in laboratory sciences, detecting batch effects may require very detailed metadata (e.g., the order of the samples, the day of testing, calibration settings), and this information is not always retained by researchers or included in publications.
            • Results Reproducibility: it consists in obtaining the same results from an independent study using the same methodologies.
              • Example:  independent scientists trying to replicate the creation of pluripotent stem cells or cold fusion under controlled circumstances to get the same the result.
            • Inferential Reproducibility: it consists in drawing qualitatively similar conclusions from either an independent replication of a study or a reanalysis of the original study. 
              • Example: trying to replicate 100 psycology studies, the interpretation of the different results let to debate among the scientists. 
                
            Group B: Tommaso, Peize, Yihua, working on Peels & Bouter ([25] in the article above)
            • Reanalysis: you take the same existing dataset from the original study and analyze it again, independently, to see whether the same results or conclusions hold: this checks whether the original analysis was sound
              • Example: A modern biologist takes Mendel’s published pea data and recalculates whether the offspring numbers match the expected dominant/recessive ratios
            • Direct Replication: you collect new data, but follow the same study protocol as closely as possible, same question, same methods, similar conditions: this tests whether the original result repeats when the study is run again
              • Example: a researcher crosses a pure-breeding tall pea plant with a pure-breeding short pea plant, then breeds the F1 generation and checks whether the F2 offspring come out in about a 3 tall : 1 short ratio, just as Mendel reported
            • Conceptual Replication: you collect new data using a modified protocol, the core question stays the same, but the methods, measures, sources, or design change: this is useful because it checks whether the finding holds across different ways of studying it
              • Example: instead of pea plants, a scientist breeds fruit flies for red eyes vs white eyes, or mice for coat color, to see whether offspring also follow predictable inheritance ratios (Morgan)
                
            Group C: Chen, Regina, Shiho, Yang, working on Leonelli ([22] in the article above)
            • Computational Reproducibility: based on prediction that given an input, and the same algorithm, the output will be the same. 
              • Example: machine learning, computational linguistics (mainly for computer science research).
            • Direct Experimental Reproducibility: when there is a strong similarity between the results of different experiments, with control over environmental variables. 
              • Example: clinical trials
            • Scoping Reproducibility (also indirect or hypothetical reproducibility): when the control over experimental conditions is extremely limited. 
              • Example: psychology, neuroscience, climate change - different studies and different methods still reaching similar conclusions.
            • Reproducible Expertise: where the researchers’ “skills and interpretative abilities are reproducible. 
              • Example: the example of the digital twin of the exhibition of the paper by Barzaghi et al. (2025).
            • Reproducible Obvservation: research in medical, historical, social sciences 
            • Subjectivity and context-dependence (rejecting the idea of reproducibility)
              • Example: Literature, and media analysis
